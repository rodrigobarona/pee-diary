## 1. EU MDR: when is a bladder/symptom diary app a medical device, what does MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1 say, which features push it to Class I / IIa, and does bundling change qualification of the whole app?

### Takeaway
Under MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1 (June 2025) a diary that only stores, displays and communicates user-entered data, with no medical intended purpose claimed, is not medical device software (MDSW); once it interprets data for an individual (flags, thresholds, suggestions, AI summaries used for clinical decisions) it is MDSW and Rule 11 makes Class IIa the default, with Class I a narrow exception. Qualification is per module, not per app, so bundling (Option B) does not by itself make the whole app a device, but Rev.1 tightened the module section: the manufacturer must delineate and document boundaries, and host-app functions the MDSW module depends on are pulled into its safety/usability assessment.

Primary text read. — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1 PDF](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf); [EC update notice 17 June 2025](https://health.ec.europa.eu/latest-updates/update-mdcg-2019-11-rev1-qualification-and-classification-software-regulation-eu-2017745-and-2025-06-17_en)
- The guidance is not legally binding: "Any views expressed in this document are not legally binding and only the Court of Justice of the European Union can give binding interpretations of Union law." — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)

**Qualification: store/display/communicate vs analyse**
- Decision step 3 (verbatim): "if the software does perform an action on data, or performs an action beyond storage, archival, communication, simple search, lossless compression ... then it may be a MDSW ... proceed to step 4." Step 4: "is the action for the benefit of individual patients?" — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1, s.3.2 decision steps](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)
- Non-medical software (verbatim): "software only intended for non-medical purposes (excluding MDR Annex XVI devices), such as invoicing, staff planning, e-mailing, web or voice messaging, data parsing, word processing, and back-up, wellness or fitness apps, do not qualify as MDSW." — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1, s.3.1](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)
- What does qualify (verbatim): "software which is intended to process, analyse, interpret, calculate, create or modify medical information may be qualified as a MDSW if the creation or modification of that information is governed by a medical intended purpose", including "software which alters the representation of data for a medical purpose". Conversely "altering the representation of data for embellishment/cosmetic or compatibility purposes does not readily qualify the software as MDSW." — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1, s.3.1](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)
- "Simple search" loses its exemption "if it contributes to achieving a medical purpose", e.g. a search "using Natural Language Processing (NLP) where those actions contribute to achieving a medical purpose." — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1, s.3.1](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)
- Intended purpose and claims drive everything: "Crafting a well-defined and clear intended purpose is paramount ... any claims, relating to the intended medical purpose of their MDSW are supported by appropriate level of clinical evidence. If this is not the case, the software ... may not be CE marked as a medical device, nor present said claims." (cites MDR Art. 7). — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1, s.3](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)
- MDR Art. 2(1) defines a medical device as software etc. "intended by the manufacturer" for, among others, "diagnosis, prevention, monitoring, prediction, prognosis, treatment or alleviation of disease" (quoted in the guidance and in INFARMED's 2026 circular). — [Regulation (EU) 2017/745](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2017/745/oj/eng); [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1, s.2](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)
- MDSW can be lay-user facing ("may be intended to be used by healthcare professionals or laypersons"). Closest analogue to a symptom diary that crosses the line: "MDSW intended to assess, monitor, and manage depression. The MDSW provides patients with questionnaires to track their mood, symptoms, and activities. It also offers exercises and videos, which are individually chosen based on the patient's input". — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1, s.3.2 Note 4 examples](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)
- Telemedicine (Annex I d.1, verbatim): "Telemedicine that solely transfers and displays information for monitoring purposes without interpreting data does not qualify as a medical device. Additional modules such as thresholds alerts may qualify as a medical device if they are intended for medical purposes." Communication systems including "video communication systems" are "normally based on software for general purposes, and do not fall within the definition of a medical device." — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1, Annex I d) and d.1)](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)
- Record systems "used solely to replace traditional paper-based patient files, do not meet the definition of a medical device", but modules that analyse patient-specific data (e.g. flagging, suggesting dosage) do. — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1, Annex I c.1)](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)
- Johner Institute and OpenRegulatory read the guidance the same way for diary apps: apps that only transmit, display or store data are not devices; OpenRegulatory's "diary app" discussion notes the grey zone begins when a physician uses the diary for decisions. — [Johner Institute, MDR Rule 11](https://blog.johner-institute.com/regulatory-affairs/mdr-rule-11/); [OpenRegulatory, Class I under MDR](https://openregulatory.com/articles/mdr-class-i-everything-you-need-to-know)

**Classification (Rule 11)**
- Rule 11 (verbatim from the guidance): "Software intended to provide information which is used to take decisions with diagnosis or therapeutic purposes is classified as class IIa", rising to IIb/III by severity; "Software intended to monitor physiological processes is classified as class IIa" (IIb for vital parameters with immediate danger); "All other software is classified as class I." — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1, s.4.2.1](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)
- MDCG reads sub-rule 11a very broadly: the wording "describes, in very general terms, the 'mode of action' which is characteristic of all MDSW. Therefore, this sub-rule is generally applicable to all MDSW". Rev.1 added that a device "intended to prevent the risk of illnesses or pathologies by analysing physiological parameters ... can be considered as a device providing information which is used to take decisions with diagnosis purpose (potential detection of pathologies) and in this case is in class IIa." — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1, s.4.2.1](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)
- Sub-rule 11b "applies to MDSW intended to be used for monitoring any/all physiological processes and not just vital physiological processes". — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1, s.4.2.1](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)
- Class I examples in Annex IV are narrow: a conception-support app that calculates fertility status from user-entered BBT and menstruation days with a validated algorithm (Class I, Rule 11c), and a symbol-to-speech communication aid. By contrast "Diagnostic MDSW intended for scoring depression based on inputted data on a patient's symptoms" is Class IIb, and software ranking therapeutic suggestions for a professional is Class IIa. — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1, Annex IV](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)
- Class IIa and above require a notified body; Class I does not (INFARMED: the conformity procedure "requer a intervenção de ... um organismo notificado europeu, sempre que o dispositivo esteja abrangido por uma classe de médio ou elevado risco (IIa, IIb ou III)"). — [INFARMED Circular Informativa 078/CD/100.20.200, 29 July 2026](https://www.infarmed.pt/documents/15786/12277909/Solu%C3%A7%C3%B5es+digitais+em+sa%C3%BAde+enquanto+dispositivos+m%C3%A9dicos/287ef796-f7d5-ff11-1f48-403825ec9378)

**Modules and bundling (s.7, substantially expanded in Rev.1)**
- "Modules that claim a medical purpose fall within the scope of the Medical Devices Regulations ... Modules without a medical purpose are not subject to these requirements." Listed non-MDSW modules include "Collect and maintain administrative patient data", "Store patient medical history", "Perform invoicing". — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1, s.7](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)
- But: "where medical device modules are designed to function in combination with other modules, whether part of the same software application or through interfacing with other devices or platforms, the overall configuration, including the connection system and interoperability aspects, must be demonstrably safe". — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1, s.7](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)
- "It is the responsibility of the manufacturer to clearly delineate the boundaries and interfaces of the various modules. Modules subject to MDR or IVDR must be explicitly identified", communicated to users including "Exactly which modules constitute the product" and "Whether the product or any of its modules are subject to the MDR/IVDR or under other applicable legislation (e.g. European Health Data Space Regulation)." — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1, s.7](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)
- "Where not all modules serve a direct medical purpose (e.g., patient record management, scheduling, or communications), but these non-medical functionalities are essential to the medical purpose of an MDSW ... Non-medical functionalities should not be excluded from the MDSW description if they are necessary for the operation of the MDSW". Example given: an MDSW extension operating through a non-device host platform's UI: "the manufacturer must assess the host module's interface as part of the MDSW's usability and clinical performance evaluations." — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1, s.7](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)
- Change control (s.8): "A change to or the addition of functionality to a software may lead it to be qualified as MDSW ... a module that is added to a software might be qualified as a MDSW on its own." — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1, s.8](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)

### Inferences
- Feature ladder for a bladder diary (my application of the texts above; not confirmed by any authority for this product):
  - **Not MDSW (with wellness/record-keeping intended purpose and matching marketing):** logging voids, volumes, fluids, leaks, urgency; listing and charting the raw entries; simple sums/averages presented as a record; PDF export; user-initiated time-limited sharing with a chosen expert (that is "communication"); reminders to log.
  - **Borderline [NB/INFARMED]:** computing clinically named indices (e.g. nocturnal polyuria index, functional bladder capacity, 24-hour frequency against "normal" ranges). This is "calculate" for an individual; it qualifies only if governed by a medical intended purpose, but colour-coding against normal ranges looks like a medical purpose in practice.
  - **MDSW, most likely Class IIa under 11a (possibly 11b "monitoring of physiological processes"):** "you may have overactive bladder" flags; threshold alerts ("your frequency is abnormal, see a specialist"); bladder-training or fluid-management suggestions personalised from entries; AI summaries for clinicians that interpret, highlight abnormalities or rank likely causes; triage that routes users to a specialty based on their data. Class I (11c) is hard to argue because MDCG treats 11a as applying to essentially all MDSW that informs diagnosis/therapy, and a flag that prompts a clinical visit is diagnostic information.
  - An AI summary that only condenses entries verbatim (no interpretation, no highlighting of abnormality) is closer to "altering representation", but an LLM summary is hard to guarantee as non-interpretive; the NLP "simple search" caveat shows MDCG's scepticism. Treat any AI summary for clinicians as presumptively MDSW until advised otherwise. **[NB/INFARMED]**
- Marketing claims matter as much as code. "Track your bladder habits and bring a clear record to your appointment" is a record-keeping claim; "monitor your overactive bladder" or "detect incontinence patterns" is a medical-purpose claim (MDR Art. 2(1) "monitoring ... of disease"). INFARMED's July 2026 circular lists "aplicações que acompanham parâmetros de saúde" among examples of devices (see Question 2), so wording discipline is essential in either option.
- Bundling effect (A vs B): the MDR status attaches to the module, so Option B does not convert booking/video/messaging into a device, and video/messaging/booking are expressly non-device functions. However, if the diary module ever becomes MDSW:
  - In B, the shared shell (login, navigation, notifications, data sync, the expert-sharing flow) becomes "host module" functionality that must be covered in the MDSW's usability, risk and change-control documentation, and every release of the combined app needs an MDR change-impact assessment (s.7, s.8). Release cadence of the marketplace is then coupled to a QMS.
  - In A, the device boundary is the diary app binary; the marketplace app ships independently.
  - In B the cross-feature data flows themselves raise qualification risk: a combined app naturally invites "based on your diary, we recommend booking X" logic, which is individualised interpretation. In A the same logic would need an explicit cross-app data flow, which makes the decision visible and deliberate.
- If no medical-purpose features are ever planned, MDR is not a discriminator between A and B; it becomes one only as an option-value argument (A keeps a future Class I/IIa path cheaper and isolates store "medical device" labelling, see Question 5).

## 2. How do INFARMED and other EU authorities treat wellness and diary apps, and which Portuguese telehealth rules (ERS, Ordem dos Fisioterapeutas) apply to an app carrying video sessions?

### Takeaway
INFARMED issued a dedicated circular on 29 July 2026 that tracks the MDCG line (booking, messaging and wellness/fitness apps are not devices; apps that "follow health parameters" or support clinical decisions are) and signals active market surveillance of apps. Separately, ERS treats any remote provision of healthcare as a "telemedicine unit" that must be registered before operating; the registrant is whoever presents itself to users as responsible for the care, "notably by issuing its own invoices or receipts", which is the key structuring question for a Stripe Connect marketplace. These ERS duties attach to the care function and are the same in A and B.

**ERS (Entidade Reguladora da Saúde)**

- Scope limits: ERS does not regulate "os profissionais de saúde no que respeita à sua atividade sujeita à regulação e disciplina das respetivas associações públicas profissionais"; territorial scope is mainland Portugal. The Ordem dos Fisioterapeutas appears among the professional bodies selectable when registering professionals. — [ERS, Manual 2025](https://www.ers.pt/media/2kskt534/manual-do-procedimento-de-registo-2025.pdf)
- ERS Alerta de Supervisão n.º 7/2024 (2 September 2024) addressed to all healthcare providers offering teleconsultations requires: (a) adequate information and prior "consentimento informado, livre e esclarecido" for care by teleconsultation; (b) adequate conditions (lighting, acoustics, location of professional and user); (c) privacy during the teleconsultation; (d) user access to clinical information produced, "que deve também ser registada no respetivo processo clínico"; (e) proof of attendance on request; (f) right to be accompanied under Lei n.º 15/2014. It cites fines under Art. 61(1)(a) of the ERS Statutes of EUR 750 to 3,740.98 or EUR 1,000 to 44,891.81 for the TMRG-related infringements. Primary text read. — [ERS Alerta de Supervisão 7/2024](https://ers.pt/media/kgahrh2u/alerta-supervisão07-2024-teleconsultas.pdf)
- The ERS study behind the alert found 34.5% of providers did not record teleconsultation information in the clinical record (search-result summary of the study). — [ERS Estudo Teleconsultas, July 2024](https://ers.pt/media/bmfey0rn/estudo-teleconsultas07-24.pdf)
- ERS "Guia Prático - Prestação de Teleconsultas" (2024) adds: inform the user about costs, alternatives and "eventual gravação de som e imagem"; record in the clinical file that information was given; attach written consent to the clinical file; verify identity of professional and user at the start; prefer authenticated institutional contact channels; produce and file a report after the session. Primary text read (partially). — [ERS Guia Prático](https://ers.pt/media/0grngycb/guia-prático_ers_-prestacao_teleconsultas.pdf)
- Cuatrecasas (21 October 2024): telemedicine units "are considered healthcare establishments and must register with ERS", are subject to general provider obligations, but have no specific regime of technical and operating requirements; the alert is "a first significant step", not a full framework. — [Cuatrecasas](https://www.cuatrecasas.com/pt/portugal/farmaceutico-saude/art/regulamentacao-das-unidades-de-telemedicina-primeiros-passos)

### Inferences
- The ERS "entidade responsável" test turns on who presents as the provider and who issues the receipt. In a Stripe Connect marketplace where each independent physiotherapist is the merchant of record and issues their own receipt, the natural reading is that each expert (a "profissional liberal") is the registrant for their own telemedicine unit (reduced fee EUR 200 + EUR 25/year if part-time and solo), and Eleva is an intermediary. If Eleva brands the service as its own care, sets clinical protocols, or issues the invoices, Eleva itself looks like the responsible entity and would need to register (minimum EUR 1,000 + EUR 500/year, scaling with the number of professionals). **[LAWYER]** This is the single most important Portuguese structuring question and it is independent of the A/B choice.
- App scope changes the perimeter, not the duty: in A the diary app sits entirely outside ERS's perimeter (no care is delivered in it); in B the same binary delivers care, so the app as a whole is the visible "telemedicine unit" front end and its consent, identity-check, recording-notice and clinical-record flows are what ERS would inspect.
- The ERS requirement that teleconsultation information be recorded in the expert's clinical record interacts with diary sharing: once an expert copies shared diary data into their record, it persists under the expert's own retention duties after the user's time-limited share expires (see Question 3).
- INFARMED's phrase "aplicações que acompanham parâmetros de saúde" is broader than MDCG's wording and would be the hook for a Portuguese surveillance query about a bladder tracker marketed with disease language. A short written GARC/DPS query describing the intended purpose is cheap insurance in either option.

### Gaps
- No Ordem dos Fisioterapeutas regulation or guidance on telephysiotherapy/digital practice was found (results surfaced only Brazilian and Colombian rules and the Ordem's general statute amended by Lei n.º 71/2023). Whether the Ordem imposes conditions on remote first assessments, advertising by members on marketplaces, or fee-splitting with platforms is unverified. **[LAWYER / ask the Ordem]**
- I found no ERS position specifically on marketplaces/intermediary platforms for independent professionals; the "entidade responsável" reading above is inference from the registration manual.
- Positions of other national authorities on diary apps (e.g. BfArM, ANSM, AEMPS) were not researched; the MDCG text is the common EU reference.
- Cross-border care (experts or users outside Portugal) and the applicable professional rules were not researched.

## 3. GDPR: what changes between (i) local-only/no account, (ii) synced to an account, (iii) shared with an expert; and does a separate app make consent and purpose limitation cleaner?

### Takeaway
Bladder data is Article 9 health data in every scenario where Eleva processes it; the legal step-change is at the moment data leaves the device. A strictly local-only diary gives Eleva a credible "we do not process your data" position (not a formal household-exemption for Eleva), while sync makes Eleva a controller needing explicit consent and, under CNPD's list, a mandatory DPIA; sharing with an expert adds a second controller with independent clinical-record duties. A separate diary app makes the consent story and the store privacy label cleaner, but only if it is genuinely telemetry-free and the sync/share steps are separate, explicit opt-ins.

### Cited Findings

**Special-category status and breadth**
- GDPR Art. 9(1) prohibits processing "data concerning health" unless an Art. 9(2) condition applies; Art. 9(2)(a) is explicit consent "for one or more specified purposes"; Art. 9(2)(h) covers provision of health care by or under the responsibility of a professional bound by secrecy (Art. 9(3)). Statute, not re-fetched. — [Regulation (EU) 2016/679](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng)
- CJEU C-21/23 Lindenapotheke (4 October 2024): order data for pharmacy-only, non-prescription medicines is health data under Art. 9(1), applying a probability/inference approach; competitors may sue over the breach as an unfair commercial practice; the German BGH applied it on 23 January 2025 (I ZR 222/19). — [Taylor Wessing](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2024/10/ecj-lindenapotheke); [Bird & Bird](https://cm.twobirds.com/en/insights/2024/global/feeling-unwell-after-the-cjeus-lindenapotheke-decision)
- Norway (EEA, GDPR applies): the Borgarting Court of Appeal on 21 October 2025 upheld Datatilsynet's NOK 65 million fine on Grindr, finding no valid consent for disclosure to advertising partners and that app-use information itself is special-category data. — [Forbrukerrådet](https://www.forbrukerradet.no/news-in-english/grindr-loses-appeal/); [EDPB summary of the original decision](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/node/2607_en)

**(i) Local-only, no account**
- GDPR Recital 18: the Regulation does not apply to processing by a natural person "in the course of a purely personal or household activity", but it "applies to controllers or processors which provide the means for processing personal data for such personal or household activities." — [GDPR Recital 18](https://gdpr-info.eu/recitals/no-18/)
- EDPB statement on smart glasses (13 October 2026), as summarised in search results and by Reed Smith: producers and app providers cannot shift GDPR obligations such as transparency onto users; "you might be a controller". Statement not read in full. — [EDPB statement on smart glasses](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2026-10/20261310_edpb_statement_smart-glasses_en.pdf); [Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/our-insights/blogs/technology-law-dispatch/102o5i8/smart-glasses-look-closelyyou-might-be-a-controller-and-the-edpb-is-watching/)
- Apple's definition supports the same factual line: "Data that is processed only on device is not 'collected' and does not need to be disclosed". — [Apple, App Privacy Details](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/app-privacy-details/)

**(ii) Synced to an Eleva account**
- CNPD Regulamento n.º 1/2018 (published as Regulamento n.º 798/2018, Diário da República 2.ª série n.º 231, 30 November 2018) lists processing that requires a DPIA in addition to GDPR Art. 35(3). Item 1 (verbatim): "Tratamento de informação decorrente da utilização de dispositivos eletrónicos que transmitam, por redes de comunicação, dados pessoais relativos à saúde". Item 2: "Interconexão de dados pessoais ou tratamento que relacione dados pessoais previstos no n.º 1 do artigo 9.º ...". Item 9: Art. 9 data "com utilização de novas tecnologias ou nova utilização de tecnologias já existentes". The list is expressly non-exhaustive. Primary text read. — [CNPD Regulamento 798/2018 (DR copy)](https://omcentro.com/ficheiros/docs/r_798_2018.pdf)
- GDPR Art. 35 (DPIA), Art. 25 (data protection by design and by default), Art. 5(1)(b)-(c) (purpose limitation, minimisation), Art. 7(3) (withdrawal as easy as giving consent), Art. 28 (processor contracts, relevant for hosting, Daily, Stripe, error-monitoring vendors). Statute, not re-fetched. — [Regulation (EU) 2016/679](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng)

**(iii) Shared with a chosen expert**
- ERS requires clinical information from teleconsultations to be recorded in the user's clinical record held by the provider. — [ERS Alerta de Supervisão 7/2024](https://ers.pt/media/kgahrh2u/alerta-supervisão07-2024-teleconsultas.pdf)
- EHDS Art. 48 gives a legislative model for user-controlled sharing from wellness apps: no automatic sharing, sharing only after consent, and the user "is able to choose which categories of health data from the wellness application are to be inserted" and the circumstances of sharing. — [EHDS Art. 48 (Ringholm text)](https://www.ringholm.com/ehds/article-48.htm)

**Store rules that mirror GDPR**
- Apple 5.1.1(ii): "Apps that collect user or usage data must secure user consent for the collection ... Apps must also provide the customer with an easily accessible and understandable way to withdraw consent." 5.1.1(iii): "Apps should only request access to data relevant to the core functionality of the app". — [App Store Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)

### Inferences
- **Scenario (i), local-only.** The "household exemption" belongs to the user, not to Eleva; Recital 18 keeps providers of the means in scope *to the extent they are controllers or processors*. If the app transmits nothing (no account, no analytics, no crash reports, no remote config tied to an identifier), the stronger argument is that Eleva performs no processing of personal data at all and so is not a controller for diary content. This is a respected but not regulator-confirmed position; the October 2026 EDPB smart-glasses statement shows regulators resist providers disclaiming responsibility for design choices. Practical consequence: Eleva should still apply Art. 25-style design (local encryption, app lock, clear in-app notice, no silent backups to Eleva) and document why it is not a controller. **[LAWYER]**
  - Any SDK that phones home breaks scenario (i): crash/performance monitoring and product analytics transmit device identifiers and usage events from an app whose mere use reveals a bladder condition (Grindr and Lindenapotheke logic). That makes Eleva a controller of at least usage data, arguably health-revealing, and triggers ePrivacy Art. 5(3) consent for non-essential access to the device. A truly "Data Not Collected" diary therefore needs zero third-party telemetry or strictly on-device diagnostics.
- **Scenario (ii), sync.** Eleva is controller. Lawful basis: Art. 6(1)(b) contract (or consent) plus Art. 9(2)(a) explicit consent; Art. 9(2)(h) is doubtful for Eleva's own sync service because Eleva is a marketplace, not the treating professional. **[LAWYER]** A DPIA is effectively mandatory in Portugal (CNPD list item 1 squarely describes a phone app transmitting health data). Other obligations: processor agreements, EU hosting/transfer mapping, retention and deletion, breach notification, DPO assessment (Art. 37(1)(c) "large scale" special-category processing is a judgement call at early scale).
- **Scenario (iii), share with expert.** The independent licensed expert is a separate controller for their clinical record (professional and ERS duties), with their own Art. 9(2)(h) basis. Eleva's disclosure to the expert rests on the user's explicit, specific, time-limited consent. Roles for the platform-held copy (Eleva as independent controller, joint controller with the expert, or processor for the expert's record tooling) depend on product design and contracts. **[LAWYER]** The UI must tell users that revoking or expiry stops access to the live diary but does not erase what the expert lawfully recorded.
- **Does a separate app make consent and purpose limitation cleaner? Mostly yes:**
  - In A the default state is "no account, nothing leaves the phone"; creating an account, turning on sync, and sharing with an expert are three distinct affirmative acts, each mapping to one purpose. That is the textbook shape for "specific" and "granular" explicit consent.
  - In B the user typically already has an account (needed for booking and payment), so diary entries are attributable to an identified customer the moment they are stored server-side; the diary purpose (self-tracking) sits beside commerce, messaging and marketing purposes in one data estate, and the temptation and the suspicion of cross-use are both higher. B can replicate A's architecture (a local-only diary module inside the app with separate sync/share opt-ins), but it must then prove internally that analytics, recommendation and CRM systems never touch diary data, and it cannot display a "Data Not Collected" label (Question 5).
  - One DPIA can cover both designs; B's DPIA is larger because it must analyse linkage risks between health diary, payment, messaging and video metadata (CNPD list item 2, interconnection of Art. 9 data).
  - A has a cost: one account spanning two apps needs consistent consent state, deletion and data-subject-rights handling across both, and users must understand that "optional sync" moves them from scenario (i) to (ii).

### Gaps
- EDPB/CNIL guidance specific to mobile apps and local-only storage (e.g. CNIL's 2024 mobile-app recommendations) could not be searched in this session; the local-only "not a controller" position is therefore supported only by Recital 18, Apple's definition and inference.
- No CNPD decision or guidance specific to health/tracker apps was found. Portuguese implementing law (Lei n.º 58/2019, notably its health-data provisions) was not reviewed. **[LAWYER]**
- EDPB Guidelines 05/2020 (consent) and 07/2020 (controller/processor) were not re-read; the granularity and role analysis above reflects their generally understood content, not verified quotes.
- Whether children/minors could use the diary (Art. 8 GDPR; Portuguese age of digital consent) was not researched.

## 4. European Health Data Space (Regulation (EU) 2025/327): wellness-app labelling, interoperability, timelines, and relevance to a diary that feeds data to professionals

### Takeaway
EHDS wellness-app obligations bite only if the manufacturer *claims interoperability with an EHR system*; then a label, common-specification conformity and registration are required, with application from 26 March 2029 (first priority data categories) and 26 March 2031 (second set). A diary that exports a PDF or shares data with an Eleva expert inside Eleva's own platform is not making that claim, so EHDS creates no near-term obligation in either option; it is a 2029+ strategic consideration.

### Cited Findings
- Definition (Art. 2, point "ab" in the consolidated text consulted): a wellness application is "any software, or any combination of hardware and software, intended by the manufacturer to be used by a natural person, for the processing of electronic health data, specifically for providing information on the health of natural persons, or the delivery of care for purposes other than the provision of healthcare." — [EHDS Art. 2 (Ringholm text)](https://www.ringholm.com/ehds/article-2.htm)
- Art. 47(1): labelling is triggered "Where a manufacturer of a wellness application claims interoperability with an EHR system". The label states the data categories confirmed compliant with Annex II essential requirements, references the common specifications, and its validity "shall not exceed three years"; for software-only apps "the label shall have a digital format"; format/content to be set by Commission implementing act; market surveillance authorities check compliance; distributors must make the label available at the point of sale in electronic form. — [EHDS Art. 47 (Ringholm text)](https://www.ringholm.com/ehds/article-47.htm)
- Art. 48: "Manufacturers of wellness applications may claim interoperability with an EHR system" if specifications are met and must "duly inform users"; interoperability "shall not entail the automatic sharing of all or part of the health data"; sharing only in accordance with Art. 5 after consent; the person must be able to choose which categories are inserted and in which circumstances. — [EHDS Art. 48 (Ringholm text)](https://www.ringholm.com/ehds/article-48.htm)
- Timeline (Art. 105): general application from 26 March 2027; Articles 3-15, 23(2)-(6), 25, 26, 27, 47, 48 and 49 apply from 26 March 2029 for the priority categories in Art. 14(1)(a)-(c) and from 26 March 2031 for Art. 14(1)(d)-(f); Chapter III for in-house/as-a-service EHR systems from 26 March 2031. — [EHDS Art. 105 (Ringholm text)](https://www.ringholm.com/ehds/article-105.htm); [Regulation (EU) 2025/327 on EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2025/327/oj/eng)
- Publication and entry into force: published in the Official Journal on 5 March 2025; Arnold & Porter gives entry into force as 25 March 2025 and first application as 25 March 2027, whereas the Art. 105 text and EU Monitor use 26 March dates. Treat 26 March as the operative date and note the one-day discrepancy between sources. — [Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2025/03/european-health-data-space-regulation-published); [EU Monitor](https://www.eumonitor.eu/9353000/1/j9vvik7m1c3gyxp/vmlg5gptv7zp)
- Heuking's summary confirms the scope: wellness apps fall under labelling and information requirements when the manufacturer claims interoperability with EHR systems. — [Heuking](https://www.heuking.de/en/news-events/newsletter-articles/detail/eu-regulation-on-the-european-health-data-space-ehds-published.html)
- MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1 cross-links the regimes: module delineation must tell users whether modules are under MDR/IVDR "or under other applicable legislation (e.g. European Health Data Space Regulation)", and "If interoperability between an MDSW and an EHR system is claimed under the ... EHDS ... manufacturers must ensure compliance with the requirements of both". — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1, s.7 and Annex I c.1)](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)
- "Data from wellness applications" is among the minimum categories of health data covered by the regulation's secondary-use framework (application from 2029). — [Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2025/03/european-health-data-space-regulation-published)

### Inferences
- Eleva Diary fits the wellness-application definition in both options (software used by a natural person to process health data to inform them about their health). The label is not a general duty on wellness apps; it is conditional on an interoperability claim. A PDF export and an in-platform share to a chosen expert are not claims of interoperability with a harmonised EHR system.
- If Eleva later wants diary data to flow into clinicians' EHR systems (e.g. via the European exchange format), a standalone diary app is the cleaner unit to label and register (one product, one label with a 3-year validity); in B the label would attach to a module of a large app and the Rev.1 module-delineation duty would apply by analogy.
- Art. 48's "no automatic sharing, user chooses categories and circumstances" is a good design template for the time-limited expert-sharing feature today, and builds forward compatibility.
- Secondary-use exposure (health data holders making data available via health data access bodies from 2029) could reach Eleva for synced diary data; a local-only diary holds no data and is unaffected. This favours A's default architecture slightly. **[LAWYER]** on whether Eleva would be a "health data holder" and whether micro/small-enterprise exemptions apply.

### Gaps
- Art. 49 (registration of wellness applications in the EU database) text was not retrieved; the registration duty for labelled apps is stated from general knowledge of the regulation.
- The Art. 47(3) implementing act on label format had not been located; status as of November 2026 unknown.
- Art. 14(1) category list (patient summaries, ePrescriptions, eDispensations in the first wave; imaging, lab results, discharge reports in the second) is from general knowledge of the regulation, not re-fetched.
- The EUR-Lex page could not be parsed in this session; article texts were read from a third-party consolidated copy (Ringholm) and should be confirmed against EUR-Lex before quotation.

### Inferences
- **"Data Not Collected" is narrower than the brief assumes.** It is available only to a diary build that transmits nothing: no account, no sync, no off-device crash/analytics data. A diary app with *optional* account sync does not meet the optional-disclosure test (ongoing sync of diary entries is part of primary functionality and is not an "each time" affirmative submission), so its label must declare Health data, linked to the user, for App Functionality. The same goes for server-mediated sharing with an expert. Sharing by PDF through the system share sheet is not collection by the app. Decision implication: either (a) ship the diary as truly local-only (export/share by file) and keep sync in the care app, earning "Data Not Collected"; or (b) accept a "Health - linked to you" label in the diary app and compete on a short, clean label. Option B can never show "Data Not Collected": one label covers the whole app and will list health, contact, identifiers, purchase/financial info, user content (messages) and diagnostics.
- **5.1.3(ii) and backups.** A local-only diary cannot use iCloud/CloudKit as its sync or backup store for health entries ("may not store personal health information in iCloud"). Device-to-device continuity therefore needs export/import files, or Eleva-hosted sync (which moves the user to GDPR scenario (ii)). Whether inclusion of the app container in the user's ordinary iCloud device backup is caught is unclear; excluding the health database from backup is the conservative choice. **[check with App Review]**
- **Account-not-required (5.1.1(v)).** A's diary satisfies this natively. In B, booking and payments are "significant account-based features", so login is justified for those; but a diary that works without an account elsewhere in the market gives a reviewer grounds to ask why the diary tab is gated. B should allow diary use before sign-in to be safe.
- **Account deletion.** Applies wherever account creation is possible: in A both apps (if the diary supports optional sign-in) need an in-app deletion path for the shared Eleva account, with clear consequences (bookings, receipts the expert must retain, synced diary). B needs it once.
- **4.8.** If either app offers Google/Facebook sign-in, it must also offer an equivalent privacy-preserving option (Sign in with Apple satisfies the three criteria); an email-only or passkey-only Eleva account avoids 4.8 entirely. Same in A and B; in A the choice must be implemented twice.
- **4.2 / 4.3 exposure in A.** A diary with logging, charts and PDF export is a self-sufficient utility, so 4.2 risk is low. Risk rises if (a) the diary's main screens are promotions for the care app (4.2.2), or (b) the care app also contains the full diary, making the standalone diary look like a second bundle ID of the same functionality (4.3(a)). Mitigation: distinct purpose, distinct metadata, and avoid a duplicate diary inside the care app (deep-link instead). Apple's own nudge ("consider submitting a single app") is the main store-policy argument for B.
- **Medical-device declaration.** Both apps in A (Health & Fitness or Medical category) and the single app in B must answer the question; "No" is correct while no module is MDSW. The store status is per app listing, while MDR status is per module. If the diary module later becomes MDSW, in B the whole Eleva app listing would carry "regulated medical device" status and regulatory contact/safety information, even though booking, video and lectures are not devices; in A only the diary listing would. A wrong "No" is a public misstatement that INFARMED (which lists app stores as a surveillance focus) could cite.
- **1.4.1 scrutiny** tracks the same features that trigger MDR (interpretation, diagnosis-like outputs). Both options should include a "check with a clinician" reminder. B attracts 1.4.1 and 5.1.1(ix) attention on first review because it brokers medical consultations (expect requests for evidence that experts are licensed and that Eleva is the legal entity offering the service).
- **HealthKit** is optional for a bladder diary (HealthKit has limited relevant types, e.g. water intake). Using it adds 5.1.2(vi)/5.1.3 constraints and a purpose-string review; in B those constraints sit in an app that also does marketing and commerce, which raises the bar for demonstrating separation.
- **Review blast radius.** In B a rejection on any dimension (payments, health claims, account deletion) blocks releases of diary, booking and video together. In A the diary can ship and iterate on a simple review profile while the marketplace app handles the harder payment/regulated-service review.

- The App Store Connect help article "Declare regulated medical device status" (exact fields, whether contact/safety details are shown publicly, and how a multi-function app with one device module should answer) was not read.

## 6. Payment rules: Apple 3.1.3(d) vs 3.1.1, EU DMA terms and fees as of 2026, Google Play equivalents, and how putting academy lectures in the same app affects commission exposure

### Takeaway
Paid 1:1 real-time sessions are outside mandatory in-app purchase on both stores (Apple 3.1.3(d); Google's 1:1 service and "regulated clinical services" exclusions), so Stripe Connect is permitted for bookings in either option. Recorded lectures, courses and content subscriptions are digital goods that fall under IAP/Play Billing or, in the EU, the commissioned alternatives. Commission is assessed per transaction type, not per app, so adding lectures to the booking app does not make bookings commissionable; it does add StoreKit/Play Billing engineering, a second payment-review surface, and a 10-26% take on academy revenue sold in-app.

### Cited Findings

**Apple guideline text**
- 3.1.1: "If you want to unlock features or functionality within your app, (by way of example: subscriptions, in-game currencies, game levels, access to premium content, or unlocking a full version), you must use in-app purchase." — [Guidelines 3.1.1](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 3.1.3(d) Person-to-Person Services: "If your app enables the purchase of real-time person-to-person services between two individuals (for example tutoring students, medical consultations, real estate tours, or fitness training), you may use purchase methods other than in-app purchase to collect those payments. One-to-few and one-to-many real-time services must use in-app purchase." — [Guidelines 3.1.3(d)](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 3.1.3(e): physical goods or services consumed outside the app "must use purchase methods other than in-app purchase". — [Guidelines 3.1.3(e)](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 3.1.3 intro: apps in this section "cannot, within the app, encourage users to use a purchasing method other than in-app purchase, except for apps on the United States storefront and as set forth in 3.1.1(a) and 3.1.3(a)." — [Guidelines 3.1.3](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 3.1.3(b) Multiplatform Services: users may access content/subscriptions acquired on other platforms or the web "provided those items are also available as in-app purchases within the app." — [Guidelines 3.1.3(b)](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- 3.1.1(a): entitlements allow links to other purchase methods "in specific regions"; outside those (and the US storefront) apps "may not include buttons, external links, or other calls to action that direct customers to purchasing mechanisms other than in-app purchase." — [Guidelines 3.1.1(a)](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)

### Inferences
- **Bookings and paid 1:1 video (both options):** exempt from IAP on Apple (3.1.3(d) names "medical consultations") and must/need not use Play Billing on Google (1:1 not replayable; "regulated clinical services should not use Google Play's billing system"). Stripe Connect is therefore store-compliant, with no store commission, provided sessions are strictly 1:1, real-time, and **not recorded for later replay**. Group sessions (one-to-few/one-to-many) and session recordings sold or bundled as content fall back under IAP/Play Billing.
- **Academy lectures, courses, content subscriptions:** digital content. On iOS in the EU from 1 October 2026 the in-app options are IAP (26%, or 15% under the Small Business Program), a third-party processor in-app (20% / 10%), or an actionable link-out (15% / 10% on sales within 7 days). A zero-commission route is to sell lectures only on the web and not sell or link to them in the app; under 3.1.3(b) access in-app to web-purchased content is allowed only if the items are also offered via IAP, unless the app qualifies under a reader-style exception. **[verify with App Review whether a lecture library qualifies]** On Android in the EEA: 10% + 5% for subscriptions via Play Billing, 15-20% + 5% for one-off purchases on new installs, lower without the billing fee via alternative billing or external links; "consumption-only" (no in-app purchase or steering) is expressly allowed.
- **Does co-locating lectures with booking increase commission exposure?** Not on the bookings themselves: exemptions are defined by the nature of each purchase, and Apple's EU terms describe commission on "digital goods and services". But co-location has four practical effects:
  1. The app must implement and pass review for two payment regimes at once (Stripe sheet for sessions, StoreKit/Play Billing or an entitlement programme for lectures). Reviewers must be convinced each flow is correctly classified; mixed apps commonly get 3.1.1 questions on first submission.
  2. Bundles break the clean split. "Session + course" packages, memberships that include both lectures and session credits, or session recordings blur the line; the digital component drags the bundle toward IAP.
  3. The 3.1.3 anti-steering sentence and link-out commission mean any in-app promotion of web-priced academy offers must run through Apple's EU entitlement terms (15%/10% for 7 days).
  4. Reporting obligations attach (Apple requires monthly transaction reporting for alternative payment processing).
- **Scope comparison:** In A, the diary app (free, no purchases) has no payment-review surface; the marketplace app carries the same mixed-payment questions as B if it also hosts the academy. The cleanest commission posture in either option is: sessions via Stripe; academy either (i) sold on web only and consumed in-app subject to the 3.1.3(b) caveat, or (ii) sold via IAP at the Small Business rate. A third variant some publishers use is a separate academy/content surface (web or separate app) so that the booking app never contains purchasable digital content; that is an argument for more separation, not less.
- At early revenue (under USD 1 million/year in proceeds) Eleva would qualify for Apple's reduced rates (15% IAP; 10% alternative payment or link-out). The absolute commission at stake on academy content is therefore modest; the engineering and review overhead is the larger cost.

- Apple's page does not state explicitly that 3.1.3(d)/(e) transactions are outside the EU commissions; it frames all commissions around "digital goods and services". Attachment 14 of the Developer Program License Agreement should be read to confirm that 1:1 consultation payments are non-commissionable. **[LAWYER / read the DPLA]**

## 7. Consumer-health privacy enforcement lessons for tracker apps and what users expect from intimate health trackers

### Takeaway
The recurring failure in intimate-tracker cases is third-party SDKs and advertising partners receiving data that reveals health status, often from the first app launch; regulators and courts now treat even app-use metadata as sensitive. Independent reviewers reward exactly the properties of a local-first, account-optional diary, which is a market and trust argument for Option A's default architecture (or for B only if it reproduces that architecture verifiably).

### Cited Findings
- Privacy International re-tested popular period-tracking apps and published on 28 May 2025: improvements since 2019-2020 include apps that let users skip account creation and "anonymous sign-in methods that keep account data separate from period tracking data"; but most apps still processed inputted data on the provider's or third parties' servers; web-traffic analysis found many third parties (advertising and functionality) receiving device-level data that combined could identify or profile a user. PI's recommendations: collect less, be clear about who handles data, give real control, and keep data on the device where feasible; "Compliance with data protection laws should be seen as a baseline, not the benchmark." — [Privacy International, 28 May 2025](https://privacyinternational.org/long-read/5593/all-eyes-my-period-period-tracking-apps-and-future-privacy-post-roe-world); [EDRi republication](https://edri.org/our-work/all-eyes-on-my-period-period-tracking-apps-and-the-future-of-privacy-in-a-post-roe-world/)
- PI notes the trade-off of local-only storage: data is lost if the device is lost or damaged, and local storage does not protect against device search or seizure. — [Privacy International](https://privacyinternational.org/long-read/5593/all-eyes-my-period-period-tracking-apps-and-future-privacy-post-roe-world)
- Mozilla Foundation published hands-on testing of six period-tracking apps on 16 July 2026, scoring them from 10/10 to 2/10. Euki (local, no account) ranked highest; Stardust was rated 2/10 and flagged for sending data to third-party tracking services from the moment the app opens, before any information is entered. — [PPC Land summary](https://ppc.land/mozilla-rates-stardust-2-of-10-over-symptom-data-sent-to-third-parties/); [CyberInsider](https://cyberinsider.com/mozilla-study-ranks-euki-as-the-most-private-period-tracker/)
- Flo Health (brief non-EU contrast, included because the brief asks for it): in Frasco v. Flo Health (N.D. Cal.) a jury found on 1 August 2025 that Meta violated the California Invasion of Privacy Act by collecting data from the Flo app via its SDK without users' consent; Flo and Google settled before verdict; reported settlements total USD 59.5 million; damages proceedings against Meta were continuing in autumn 2026. — [HIPAA Journal](https://www.hipaajournal.com/jury-trial-meta-flo-health-cons); [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/federal-jury-finds-against-meta-collecting-data-flo-health); [Labaton case page](https://www.labaton.com/cases/frasco-v-flo-health-inc)
- EEA enforcement anchor: Grindr's NOK 65 million fine (disclosure of app-use data to advertising partners without valid consent; app-use data held to be special-category) was upheld by the Borgarting Court of Appeal on 21 October 2025 after three rounds of appeal. — [Forbrukerrådet](https://www.forbrukerradet.no/news-in-english/grindr-loses-appeal/); [EDPB](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/node/2607_en)
- CJEU Lindenapotheke (C-21/23, 4 October 2024) broadened "health data" to data from which health can be inferred and opened the door to competitor lawsuits over Art. 9 breaches. — [Taylor Wessing](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2024/10/ecj-lindenapotheke)
- CMS's GDPR Enforcement Tracker report for life sciences/healthcare: the most common reason for fines in the sector was insufficient technical and organisational measures (security/access control). — [CMS Enforcement Tracker Report](https://cms.law/en/int/publication/gdpr-enforcement-tracker-report/life-science-healthcare)
- A UK ICO poll (September 2023) found about one third of women had used period or fertility tracking apps (secondary report; UK, outside the EU frame). — [Digital Health Insights](https://dhinsights.org/news/mozilla-report-reveals-wide-privacy-gaps-among-popular-period-tracking-apps)

### Inferences
- A bladder/continence diary is at least as stigmatising as a cycle tracker; the same expectations transfer: usable without an account, data on device by default, no third-party trackers, honest store labels, easy export and deletion.
- The Grindr and Lindenapotheke reasoning means that for an app named and marketed as a bladder diary, *the fact of use* is health-revealing. An analytics or attribution SDK that sends an identifier plus "app opened" from the diary is an Article 9 problem even if no diary entry is transmitted. This applies equally to the diary module in B, where SDK separation inside one binary is harder to guarantee and to evidence.
- Reviewer scorecards (Mozilla, PI) evaluate per app. A standalone local-first diary can earn a top rating; an all-in-one commerce app with payments, messaging and marketing will be judged on its whole data footprint, whatever the diary module does.
- Competitor standing after Lindenapotheke (at least in Germany) means privacy missteps in a health marketplace can be attacked by rivals, not only regulators.
- Counterpoint for balance: PI's own caveat (data loss, device seizure) and user demand for multi-device continuity mean a local-only default should be paired with a well-explained opt-in sync or encrypted export; "local-only forever" is not what all users want.

### Gaps
- I found no 2025-2026 fine by an EU data protection authority specifically against a consumer health-tracker app for SDK/tracker sharing. A reported CNIL EUR 5 million fine on IQVIA Operations France (May 2026) appeared only on a tracker site and is unverified.
- No EU regulator "sweep" of period/fertility or continence apps was located; the UK ICO review of period apps was not verified and is outside the EU frame.
- Mozilla's 2026 review was read only via secondary summaries; the primary Mozilla page and its scoring criteria were not read.
- No user research specific to bladder-diary users' privacy expectations was found; expectations are extrapolated from reproductive-health trackers.

## 8. Marketing consent: can a free diary app lawfully promote the publisher's paid care service inside the app?

### Takeaway
Yes for generic, non-personalised promotion of Eleva's own service inside the app (the same message to every user, no use of diary data), subject to store rules that the app must not be primarily marketing. No, without explicit opt-in consent, for anything that uses diary entries or inferred health status to target offers; that combination is also where MDR risk appears, because a data-driven "you should see a pelvic physio" prompt is individualised interpretation.

### Cited Findings
- GDPR Art. 9(1)-(2): health data may be processed only under an Art. 9(2) condition; for marketing the only realistic one is explicit consent (Art. 9(2)(a)). Art. 21(2)-(3): absolute right to object to direct marketing including profiling. Statute, not re-fetched. — [Regulation (EU) 2016/679](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng)
- CJEU Lindenapotheke confirms that processing health-revealing customer data without prior explicit consent breaches Art. 9 and can be an unfair commercial practice. — [Taylor Wessing](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2024/10/ecj-lindenapotheke)
- CJEU C-252/21 Meta v Bundeskartellamt (4 July 2023) is the reference case on personalised advertising and special-category data; C-446/21 Schrems v Meta (October 2024) held that targeted advertising is not illegal per se but personal data cannot be processed indefinitely for it. — [FRA case summary, C-252/21](https://fra.europa.eu/en/node/44533); [EU Law Analysis on legitimate interest](https://eulawanalysis.blogspot.com/2024/10/latest-updates-on-legitimate-interest.html)
- EDPB Guidelines 1/2024 on legitimate interest (Art. 6(1)(f)) were adopted for consultation in October 2024 and lean on the Meta case; commentary on the draft disagrees on how far Art. 9 data can ever be processed alongside a legitimate-interest basis. The final text was not located in this session. — [EDPB Guidelines 1/2024 consultation page](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/our-work-tools/documents/public-consultations/2024/guidelines-12024-processing-personal-data-based_cs); [Inside Privacy (Covington)](https://www.insideprivacy.com/data-privacy/five-key-takeaways-from-recent-eu-developments-on-the-gdprs-legitimate-interests-legal-basis/)
- Portugal, electronic marketing: CNPD Diretriz/2022/1 (25 January 2022) on direct-marketing electronic communications under Lei n.º 41/2004 Art. 13.º-A. Without a prior customer relationship, consent is the only basis; for existing customers, marketing of the sender's own analogous products or services may proceed without consent provided an easy, free opt-out is offered at collection and in every message; consent bundled as a condition of access is invalid. CNPD received about four thousand complaints on unsolicited communications between May 2019 and January 2022. — [Garrigues](https://www.garrigues.com/en_GB/new/new-guidelines-direct-marketing-issued-portuguese-data-protection-authority-what-has-change-way); [Abreu Advogados via Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/privacy-protection/1160114/new-cnpd-guidelines-on-direct-marketing-electronic-communications); [PLMJ](https://www.plmj.com/xms/files/08_Publicacoes/019_PORTUGAL_TD.pdf)
- Apple 5.1.3(i): apps "may not use or disclose to third parties data gathered in the health, fitness, and medical research context ... for advertising, marketing, or other use-based data mining purposes other than improving health management ... and then only with permission." 5.1.2(vi): HealthKit data "may not be used for marketing, advertising or use-based data mining". — [Guidelines 5.1.3(i), 5.1.2(vi)](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- Apple 4.2.2: "apps shouldn't primarily be marketing materials, advertisements". — [Guidelines 4.2.2](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- Apple's privacy-label rules: data used for "your Advertising or Marketing purposes" can never be treated as optional-to-disclose. — [Apple, App Privacy Details](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/app-privacy-details/)
- Google Health Connect data may not be used for serving ads, including personalised advertising; new purposes require new consent. — [Google Play, sensitive permissions and APIs](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9888170?hl=en)
- MDCG: "Additional modules such as thresholds alerts may qualify as a medical device if they are intended for medical purposes." — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1, Annex I d.1)](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Three tiers of in-app promotion:**
  1. *Static, untargeted* ("Want to discuss your diary with a pelvic health specialist? Explore Eleva Care" shown to everyone in a clearly separate area): involves no processing of health data for marketing and is not an "electronic communication" in the Art. 13.º-A sense; lawful in a local-only app without consent. Keep it secondary to diary function (4.2.2).
  2. *Push notifications, email or SMS promoting Eleva Care:* electronic direct marketing. A free diary user who has bought nothing is weak ground for the "existing customer / analogous products" exception, so opt-in consent is the safe basis; Apple additionally requires explicit in-app opt-in for promotional push (guideline 4.5.4, cited from general knowledge). Whether push falls under Art. 13.º-A was not addressed in the sources found. **[LAWYER]**
  3. *Targeted using diary data or inferred condition* ("you logged 6 leaks this week, book a session"): processing health data for marketing. Requires separate explicit consent under Art. 9(2)(a) (legitimate interest cannot supply the Art. 9 condition), is restricted by Apple 5.1.3(i) to permissioned "improving health management" uses, and, because it is an individualised threshold-based prompt toward care, risks qualifying the feature as MDSW. Even if computed entirely on-device (reducing the GDPR problem), the MDR and Apple issues remain. Recommendation for scoring: treat tier 3 as out of scope unless Eleva deliberately takes the MDSW route.
- **A vs B:** In A, cross-promotion is an explicit hand-off between two products (deep link from diary to care app), and the diary's privacy label can stay free of "Marketing" purposes if only tier 1 is used. In B, the same account, the same analytics pipeline and the same CRM serve both diary and commerce; avoiding accidental tier-3 processing (e.g. lifecycle marketing segments built from "diary active users with leak entries") requires strict internal segregation and will have to be declared on the label if any diary-derived signal feeds marketing. B makes compliant *generic* promotion trivial (it is just navigation within one app) but makes *non-compliant* targeting much easier to do by accident.
- Simply knowing that an identified account uses the bladder diary is health-revealing (Question 7). In B, sending "complete your diary" lifecycle emails to diary users is defensible as service messaging; sending them physiotherapy offers because they use the diary is tier 3.

### Gaps
- The final version of EDPB Guidelines 1/2024 (legitimate interest) and its treatment of direct marketing with special-category data was not located.
- No CNPD or EDPB statement on in-app promotional surfaces or on push notifications as direct marketing was found; tier 1 and tier 2 conclusions are inference from the statutory scheme and the CNPD marketing guideline.
- The consolidated text of Lei n.º 41/2004 Art. 13.º-A/13.º-B was not read; secondary sources were inconsistent about Art. 13.º-B.
- Portuguese rules on advertising of health services (e.g. the health-advertising regime enforced by ERS) and Ordem dos Fisioterapeutas advertising rules for members were not researched and are relevant to how Eleva promotes experts. **[LAWYER]**

## 9. Overall: how does app scope (two apps vs one) change compliance risk and effort across these regimes?

### Takeaway
Scope does not change which laws apply to each function; it changes how far each regime's footprint spreads, how clean the consent and labelling story is, and how much one problem can block everything else. Option A lowers risk on privacy perception, store review and future MDR optionality at the price of duplicated store/account plumbing and a small 4.3/4.2.2 exposure; Option B lowers duplication and makes Apple's "single app" preference moot but couples every release and label to the most regulated function in the bundle.

### Cited Findings
- Module-level qualification with manufacturer-defined boundaries, and host-interface inclusion where an MDSW module depends on the host. — [MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1, s.7-8](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)
- Store declarations and labels are per app listing: Apple's regulated-medical-device status and App Privacy answers; Google's Health apps declaration and "Medical Device" identification. — [Apple Developer News, 26 March 2026](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=nyqbfz1y); [Apple, App Privacy Details](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/app-privacy-details/); [Google Play Health Content and Services](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/16679511?hl=en)
- Apple's own text nudges consolidation where apps are variants of one another ("consider submitting a single app"). — [Guidelines 4.3(a)](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- Payment exemptions are defined per purchase type (3.1.3(d) for 1:1 real-time services; Google 1:1/clinical-services carve-outs). — [Guidelines 3.1.3(d)](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/); [Google Play payments FAQ](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/10281818?hl=en)
- CNPD requires a DPIA for apps transmitting health data over networks. — [CNPD Regulamento 798/2018](https://omcentro.com/ficheiros/docs/r_798_2018.pdf)
- ERS registration attaches to whoever is responsible for remote care, regardless of app packaging. — [ERS, Manual 2025](https://www.ers.pt/media/2kskt534/manual-do-procedimento-de-registo-2025.pdf)

### Inferences

Scoring aid (all cells are my synthesis of the findings above; "risk" = likelihood x impact of a compliance problem, "effort" = work to reach and keep compliance).

| Dimension | Option A: Diary app + separate care app | Option B: one all-in-one app |
|---|---|---|
| MDR today (no interpretive features) | Neither app is MDSW if claims stay record-keeping. Equal. | Same. Equal. |
| MDR if diary later adds flags/AI summaries | Device boundary = diary app; marketplace releases unaffected; store "medical device" label only on diary listing. Lower risk, lower ongoing effort. | Module is the device but host shell, sync, notifications and sharing flows enter its technical file and change control; whole listing shows device status. Higher effort; release coupling. |
| Risk of drifting into MDSW by accident | Lower: cross-app data use must be built deliberately. | Higher: diary data sits next to booking/recommendation logic and growth experiments. |
| ERS / telehealth duties | Identical; attach to care app only. Diary app cleanly outside perimeter. | Identical duties; the single app is the telemedicine front end. |
| GDPR lawful basis and consent | Cleanest: no-account default; sync and share are separate explicit opt-ins. | Achievable but account exists by default; needs demonstrable internal segregation of diary data. |
| DPIA | Required once sync/sharing exists; smaller scope for diary; second (or combined) DPIA for marketplace. | Required; one larger DPIA including linkage risks (CNPD list items 1 and 2). |
| Apple privacy label | Diary can be "Data Not Collected" only if strictly local with zero telemetry; otherwise "Health, linked to you" but short. Care app has the long label. | One long label for everything; "Data Not Collected" impossible. |
| Apple account rules (5.1.1(v), 4.8, deletion) | Diary complies natively; shared-account deletion/login must be built in two apps. | Built once; diary should still work pre-login to avoid 5.1.1(v) pushback. |
| Apple 4.2 / 4.3 | Low but non-zero: avoid funnel-only diary and avoid duplicating the diary in the care app. | No 4.3 exposure. |
| Apple 1.4.1 / 5.1.1(ix) scrutiny | Concentrated on care app; diary review is simple. | Whole app reviewed as a medical-services app; one rejection blocks all features. |
| Payments and commission | Diary app has no purchases. Care app: sessions via Stripe (exempt); academy content raises IAP/alternative-terms questions if hosted there. | Same classification per transaction; both payment regimes must coexist in one binary; bundles and recordings blur lines. |
| Google Play | Health apps declaration and "not a medical device" disclaimer on both listings. | One declaration and disclaimer. |
| EHDS (2029+) | Diary is a clean unit to label if interoperability is ever claimed; local-only data is outside secondary-use reach. | Label would attach to a module of a large app; more data held centrally. |
| Marketing | Generic cross-promotion via deep link is clean; label can omit marketing use. | Generic promotion trivial; accidental health-based targeting risk higher. |
| Trust / external reviews | Can score top marks as a local-first tracker. | Judged on the whole commerce app's footprint. |
| Engineering/compliance duplication | Two listings, two review tracks, two privacy labels, shared-account plumbing on two platforms x two apps. | One of each. |

- Net read for a judging panel: on **compliance risk**, A is lower on privacy, store-review blast radius, accidental MDR drift and reputational exposure; B is lower only on 4.3 and on consistency errors between two apps. On **compliance effort**, B is lower at launch (one DPIA, one label, one review, one account implementation) while A is lower over time if the diary ever moves toward interpretive or AI features, and A's diary can launch earlier on a simple review profile.
- A hybrid is available and worth scoring: Option B's single app but with the diary implemented as a local-first, pre-login module with separate sync/share consents and no telemetry in that module. It captures part of A's GDPR cleanliness but none of A's store-label, review-isolation or MDR-boundary advantages.
- Items that are identical in both options and should not influence the A/B score, but must be resolved either way: the ERS "who is the responsible entity" structure; controller/processor roles with experts; the academy payment route; VAT; wording of health claims.

### Gaps
- Points needing a qualified opinion before the decision is final: (1) **[NB/INFARMED]** whether planned charts/indices/AI summaries are MDSW and in which class, and the status of the MDR reform proposal; (2) **[LAWYER]** ERS registration model for a Stripe Connect marketplace and Ordem dos Fisioterapeutas rules; (3) **[LAWYER]** controller/joint-controller/processor allocation between Eleva and experts, Art. 9 basis for sync, and the "not a controller" position for local-only use; (4) **[LAWYER / DPLA]** confirmation that 1:1 session payments are outside Apple's EU commissions and the compliant route for academy content; (5) **[App Review query]** iCloud/backup handling for local health data and how a multi-function app should answer the medical-device declaration.
- No quantitative data was found on App Review rejection rates for single-publisher companion apps or for mixed-payment health apps; those risk levels are qualitative judgements.
- This comparison assumes the academy would live in the care app (A) or the single app (B); if academy content is web-only, the payment row largely drops out for both.
